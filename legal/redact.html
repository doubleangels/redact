1. Overview

Redact is an open-source Android app, sold as a one-time purchase on Google Play, that strips hidden metadata, things like GPS location, device details, timestamps, and camera settings, out of your photos and videos. You can also peek at what's hidden inside a file before you share it, and convert between formats, all without anything ever leaving your phone.

This page explains, in plain language, exactly what Redact does (and doesn't do) with your information. Here's the short version: we built this app because we don't want your data, so we made sure it never collects any. Everything below walks through why that's true, including every permission the app asks for, every piece of outside code it uses, and the rights you have under privacy law, wherever you happen to be.

Privacy isn't a feature we bolted on, it's the reason Redact exists. We believe what's on your phone is yours, and that an app has no business watching what you do with it. That's why Redact has no analytics, no ads, no tracking, and no account, and why its code is public for anyone to check. A privacy tool that tracked its own users would defeat its whole purpose, and we'd never build one.

3. Information We Collect

Here's the plain truth: Redact doesn't collect any of your personal information. There's no account, no sign-in, no sign-up form, and no server anywhere waiting to receive your photos, videos, or identity. You don't have to take our word for it either, the INTERNET permission is the only thing that lets the app talk to the network at all, and it's used for exactly one optional purpose, covered in Section 6. The only other ways information about you reaches us are the limited order details Google Play shares when you buy Redact, explained below, and anything you choose to send us by email or on GitHub, covered in Section 18.

To be really specific, here's what Redact will never do.

  • Upload your photos, videos, or any other file to a server, for any reason.
  • Read, log, or send off the hidden metadata it scans or removes.
  • Ask for your name, email address, phone number, or any kind of account ID.
  • Use advertising identifiers, behavior tracking, or any ad software.
  • Follow you across other apps or websites, or build a profile about you.

The only thing Redact itself can ever send from your device is the optional, anonymous diagnostic data covered in Section 6, and that only happens if you turn it on yourself.

You're always in control here. Head to Apps > Redact > Permissions in your phone's Settings any time to see what's been granted and take any of it back. Turning off an optional permission just disables the one feature that needed it. Everything else in Redact keeps working normally.

5. Push Notifications

Redact can pop up a friendly notification when a job's progress updates or finishes, controlled by the Notifications setting, which starts off. These are made right there on your phone by the app itself. Nothing is being pushed in from some server out in the world.

There's no Firebase Cloud Messaging or any other push service involved here, we don't even have a server that could send you a push message, and Redact never registers a device token, push token, or advertising identifier for this or anything else. You can also mute or tweak Redact's notification channels any time from your phone's own Settings, separately from the toggle in the app.

6. Optional Crash Reporting

Sentry is in Redact for one reason only: to find bugs and errors so we can fix them and make the app better for everyone. It isn't used for analytics, marketing, or tracking, and it never will be. We don't use it to learn who you are, what you do in the app, or what's in your files, only where the app broke or ran slowly.

If you ever want to help us catch bugs, Redact has an optional crash-reporting feature powered by a service called Sentry. It lives behind a single setting, Send Crash Reports, and it starts off. Until you switch it on yourself, nothing is sent anywhere, Sentry's SDK isn't even initialized.

If you do turn it on, here's everything we do to protect your privacy before anything leaves your phone.

  • No personal info by default: we've explicitly turned off the SDK's default personal-data collection (sendDefaultPii = false), along with broad device-context collection and automatic activity tracking.
  • No user or install ID: Sentry's SDK normally tags every report with a random per-install ID. Redact removes it, so reports from the same phone can't be linked together, or back to you.
  • No screenshots, ever: screenshot and screen-layout capture are disabled, so a crash can never grab an image of whatever was on your screen.
  • Automatic scrubbing: before anything gets sent, our own filter strips out content:// and file:// paths, GPS coordinates, and file names from every message and error trace, even ones generated deep inside Android's own system code.
  • Only pre-approved labels: diagnostic tags are limited to a short, reviewed list (things like operation_type or video_width), never open-ended text that could accidentally contain something personal.
  • Your media stays untouched: Sentry never has access to the photos or videos you're working with, their contents, or the metadata inside them.

Exactly What Can Be Sent

With Send Crash Reports on, Redact can send these kinds of anonymous technical data, and nothing else.

  • Crash and error reports: a scrubbed error trace, the app version, your device model, and Android version.
  • App-not-responding reports: the same details, when the app freezes.
  • Performance data: for about one in four app sessions, how long screens took to open, slow or frozen frame counts, and timing samples of the app's own code. This shows us where Redact is slow, not what you were doing.
  • Diagnostic logs and counters: short, scrubbed messages and numbers the app records about its own operations, like how long a conversion took or how many files were in a batch.
  • A note you choose to write: after an error, Redact may offer an Add Details button. If you type a note and tap Send, that text is attached to the report. File paths and GPS coordinates are removed from it, and it never asks for your name or email. Please don't include personal information in it.

Like any connection on the internet, Sentry's servers can see the IP address a report comes from while receiving it. Redact doesn't put your IP address in the report, and we don't use it to identify anyone.

Who Handles It and Where

Sentry (Functional Software, Inc.) receives and stores this data on our behalf as a service provider (a "processor" under GDPR), on servers in the United States, under its own Data Processing Addendum. Sentry keeps it for its standard retention period for our plan, currently up to 90 days, and then deletes it automatically. See Sentry's data retention periods for details.

Because the data is anonymous, we can't find, show, or delete any one person's reports. Nothing in them tells us which reports came from you. If you want no more data sent, turn the setting off, it takes effect immediately, and the reports already sent expire on Sentry's schedule.

You're free to turn crash reporting back off whenever you like, and it stops instantly. Sentry's own handling is also covered by Sentry's Privacy Policy.

9. Where Your Files Go

By default, cleaned and converted files land in Pictures/Redact or Movies/Redact, right in your phone's regular photo library. They're ordinary files you can back up, move, or delete exactly like any other photo or video, and Redact never moves them anywhere else.

If you'd rather keep them somewhere else, Output Folders in Settings opens Android's own file browser so you can pick or create a folder for images and another for videos, on your phone's storage or an SD card. Android then lets Redact create files in that one folder, and only that folder: Redact uses this access to save your cleaned and converted copies there and nothing else, and it doesn't browse, change, or upload anything else in it. Reset to Default hands that access back to Android, and so does choosing a different folder. If the folder you picked is deleted or stops being available, Redact saves to the default folder instead and tells you so. Files already saved stay where they are either way.

When you share a file into Redact from another app, it quietly makes a cleaned temporary copy, hands it off to wherever you're sharing to, and then deletes it shortly after. If you want extra peace of mind, Settings lets you overwrite temporary files (1, 3, or 7 passes) before they're deleted, though modern phone storage limits how much that really adds, as the app itself is upfront about. Clear Cache on Startup automatically clears out anything older than 24 hours, and you can always clear it by hand from Settings too.

Your Privacy Stats at the top of Settings, and the Statistics screen it opens, are counted right there on your phone: how many files Redact has cleaned, shared, and converted, how many metadata fields and locations it removed, which kinds of personal details those files carried, and how many files you cleaned on each of the last 30 days. They're plain numbers, never your files, their names, or what the metadata said. They live in Redact's private storage, are left out of backups and device transfers, and never go into crash reports. Clear Statistics sets every count back to zero, and uninstalling Redact removes them.

When You Uninstall Redact

Android keeps every app's private data in its own sandbox, so uninstalling Redact wipes all of it instantly, your settings, your statistics, including whether crash reporting was on, and anything left in the temporary cache. None of it gets backed up to the cloud first, since we've turned that off. The exception is your actual photos and videos: anything already saved to Pictures/Redact, Movies/Redact, or a folder you chose in Settings is yours, just like photos from your camera, and uninstalling the app won't touch them. Android also withdraws Redact's access to any folder you chose.

If you'd turned crash reporting on at some point, reports already sent stay in Sentry until they expire, as described in Section 6. Since they're anonymous, uninstalling can't remove them early, and neither can we.

11. Security

Because everything happens locally and there's no account system or database anywhere, there's simply no central server holding your photos, videos, or personal information for anyone to break into. The only network connection Redact ever makes is the single, opt-in diagnostic channel from Section 6, and that's encrypted over HTTPS.

Redact also blocks all unencrypted network traffic outright (usesCleartextTraffic="false") and turns off Android's automatic app backup (allowBackup="false"), so nothing can be pulled out through a device backup. There's no password, PIN, or credential stored anywhere, since there's no account to begin with. And because the full source code is public, you don't have to take our word for any of this, anyone can go check for themselves.

No system is perfectly secure, and we can't guarantee that one never will be. If we learn of a security incident affecting information we're responsible for, we'll take reasonable steps to contain it and notify affected people and authorities where the law requires us to. Found a security problem in Redact? Please report it as described in our security policy.

13. Children's Privacy

Redact doesn't knowingly collect personal information from anyone, no matter their age. There's no account to make and no chat or social feature. The app isn't aimed at children, and its Google Play listing doesn't target kids. This is how we meet laws like the U.S. Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA), the GDPR's rules on children's consent, and the UK Age Appropriate Design Code: every feature starts with the most private setting, and nothing builds a profile of anyone.

The only ways a child could send us anything are by typing it into the optional crash-report note, or by emailing us. If you're a parent or guardian and believe your child under 13 (or under the age of digital consent where you live, up to 16 in parts of the EU) has sent us personal information, contact us using the details in Section 18 and we'll delete any of it we can find. Anonymous crash notes can't be traced to a person, so please tell us roughly what was written and when.

15. Your Rights

Legal Basis for Processing (GDPR)

Wherever the GDPR or a similar law applies, sending optional diagnostic data to Sentry is based entirely on your consent (GDPR Article 6(1)(a)), given the moment you switch on Send Crash Reports. You can withdraw that consent at any time by switching it off again, which stops any further processing immediately. It doesn't retroactively affect the lawfulness of anything already sent before you withdrew it.

If you email us, we use what you send only to read and answer your message and keep a record of the conversation. That's based on our legitimate interest in running and supporting the app (Article 6(1)(f)), or on taking steps you asked for.

We keep the order details Google Play shares with us because tax and accounting laws require it (Article 6(1)(c)), and to handle refunds and purchase problems (Article 6(1)(b)).

European Economic Area, UK, and Switzerland (GDPR)

If you're in the EEA, UK, or Switzerland, data protection law gives you the right to access, correct, delete, restrict, or move your personal data, and to object to how it's used. Since Redact doesn't collect or process your personal data in the first place, most of these rights simply won't have anything to act on, there's nothing sitting around to request. If you think some data about you has reached us anyway, say, through an email you sent, just get in touch and we'll sort it out quickly.

Crash diagnostics are a special case. They contain nothing that identifies you, so we have no way to tell which reports, if any, are yours. Under GDPR Article 11, we aren't required to collect extra information just to identify you, which means requests to access or delete specific crash reports can't be fulfilled. You can still stop all future reports by turning the setting off.

Right to Complain

If you think we've mishandled your data, please tell us first so we can try to fix it. You also have the right to complain to a data protection authority, for example the authority in your EU country of residence, the UK Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), the Swiss Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC), or your local equivalent listed below. In the United States, you can contact your state attorney general or the Federal Trade Commission.

International Data Transfers

If you turn on crash reporting, that limited diagnostic data from Section 6 might be processed by Sentry (Functional Software, Inc.) on servers outside your own country, including in the United States. This only ever happens if you've opted in, and it only ever covers that same scrubbed technical data, never your photos, videos, or identity. Sentry protects transfers like this under its Data Processing Addendum, which includes the European Commission's Standard Contractual Clauses and their UK and Swiss equivalents. If you email us, your message is handled by our email providers, which may also store it in the United States. Turning on crash reporting is your explicit consent to this transfer, including where a law like China's PIPL requires separate consent for sending data abroad.
