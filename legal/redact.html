1. Overview

Redact is a free, open-source Android app that strips hidden metadata, things like GPS location, device details, timestamps, and camera settings, out of your photos and videos. You can also peek at what's hidden inside a file before you share it, and convert between formats, all without anything ever leaving your phone.

This page explains, in plain language, exactly what Redact does (and doesn't do) with your information. Here's the short version: we built this app because we don't want your data, so we made sure it never collects any. Everything below walks through why that's true, including every permission the app asks for, every piece of outside code it uses, and the rights you have under privacy law, wherever you happen to be.

2. Who We Are

Redact is built and maintained by DoubleAngels, an independent developer, not a data company, not an ad company, and not anyone running a server full of your photos. The only data we're ever responsible for is the single, opt-in diagnostic category described in Section 6, where privacy law would consider us the data controller.

You're welcome to reach out any time using the contact details in Section 16, read every line of the app's source code on GitHub, or even look back through this very policy's edit history, since it's published straight from that same public repository.

3. Information We Collect

Here's the plain truth: we don't collect any of your personal information. There's no account, no sign-in, no sign-up form, and no server anywhere waiting to receive your photos, videos, or identity. You don't have to take our word for it either, the INTERNET permission is the only thing that lets the app talk to the network at all, and it's used for exactly one optional purpose, covered in Section 6.

To be really specific, here's what Redact will never do.

  • Upload your photos, videos, or any other file to a server, for any reason.
  • Read, log, or send off the hidden metadata it scans or removes.
  • Ask for your name, email address, phone number, or any kind of account ID.
  • Use advertising identifiers, behavior tracking, or any ad software.
  • Follow you across other apps or websites, or build a profile about you.

The only thing that can ever leave your device is the optional, anonymous crash report covered in Section 6, and that only happens if you turn it on yourself.

4. Permissions Explained

Android makes every app list its permissions up front, but just because a permission is listed doesn't mean it's used to collect anything about you. Here's exactly what each one does, and whether you actually need to grant it.

Permission What It's Used For Status
READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE Lets the system gallery picker show your photos/videos on Android 12 and earlier, where the modern document picker isn't available. Android 12 and Earlier
READ_MEDIA_IMAGES / READ_MEDIA_VIDEO Optional alternative to Android's system document picker, which Redact uses by default on Android 13+ and which needs no media permission at all. Optional
READ_MEDIA_VISUAL_USER_SELECTED Lets you (on Android 14+) grant Redact access to only specific photos/videos you choose, instead of your entire library. Optional
ACCESS_MEDIA_LOCATION Lets the Scan screen display the exact GPS coordinates embedded in a file, so you can see what's there. Not required to remove that same GPS data in Clean. Optional
POST_NOTIFICATIONS Shows progress and completion notifications for Clean/Convert jobs. Optional (Off by Default)
FOREGROUND_SERVICE / FOREGROUND_SERVICE_MEDIA_PROCESSING Keeps a long-running Clean or Convert batch job alive and shows its progress notification when notifications are enabled. Conditional (Notifications)
INTERNET The only permission that allows network access at all. Used exclusively to send scrubbed crash/error diagnostics to Sentry, and only when you turn on Send Crash Reports in Settings. Conditional (Crash Reporting)

By default, Redact never asks for broad, standing access to your whole media library. On Android 13 and up, it mainly uses Android's own built-in file picker, which only hands over the exact files you choose. Everything else in your gallery stays out of sight, as far as the app is concerned.

You're always in control here. Head to Apps > Redact > Permissions in your phone's Settings any time to see what's been granted and take any of it back. Turning off an optional permission just disables the one feature that needed it. Everything else in Redact keeps working normally.

5. Push Notifications

Redact can pop up a friendly notification when a job's progress updates or finishes, controlled by the Notifications setting, which starts off. These are made right there on your phone by the app itself. Nothing is being pushed in from some server out in the world.

There's no Firebase Cloud Messaging or any other push service involved here, we don't even have a server that could send you a push message, and Redact never registers a device token, push token, or advertising identifier for this or anything else. You can also mute or tweak Redact's notification channels any time from your phone's own Settings, separately from the toggle in the app.

6. Optional Crash Reporting

If you ever want to help us catch bugs, Redact has an optional crash-reporting feature powered by a service called Sentry. It lives behind a single setting, Send Crash Reports, and it starts off. Until you switch it on yourself, nothing is sent anywhere, Sentry's SDK isn't even initialized.

If you do turn it on, here's everything we do to protect your privacy before anything leaves your phone.

  • No personal info by default: we've explicitly turned off the SDK's default personal-data collection (sendDefaultPii = false), along with broad device-context collection and automatic activity tracking.
  • No screenshots, ever: screenshot and screen-layout capture are disabled, so a crash can never grab an image of whatever was on your screen.
  • Automatic scrubbing: before anything gets sent, our own filter strips out content:// and file:// paths, GPS coordinates, and file names from every message and error trace, even ones generated deep inside Android's own system code.
  • Only pre-approved labels: diagnostic tags are limited to a short, reviewed list (things like operation_type or video_width), never open-ended text that could accidentally contain something personal.
  • Your media stays untouched: Sentry never has access to the photos or videos you're working with. At most, a report includes your device model, Android version, app version, a scrubbed error trace, and those pre-approved tags, nothing more.

You're free to turn crash reporting back off whenever you like, and it stops instantly. Anything already sent to Sentry is covered by Sentry's own Privacy Policy.

8. Advertising, Tracking, and Automated Decision-Making

Redact shows zero ads and contains zero advertising code, so there's genuinely nothing here to opt out of. We don't use the Android Advertising ID, we don't build profiles about your interests or behavior, and we don't share or sell anything to ad networks or data brokers, mostly because we never collect the data that would make any of that possible in the first place.

There are no cookies either, Redact is a real Android app, not a website, and it doesn't track you across other apps or sites. Nothing here makes automated decisions about you or profiles you in any meaningful way. Every single action, cleaning, scanning, converting, happens because you tapped a button, and nothing about how you use the app shapes any decision made about you.

9. Where Your Files Go

Cleaned and converted files land in Pictures/Redact or Movies/Redact, right in your phone's regular photo library. They're ordinary files you can back up, move, or delete exactly like any other photo or video, and Redact never moves them anywhere else.

When you share a file into Redact from another app, it quietly makes a cleaned temporary copy, hands it off to wherever you're sharing to, and then deletes it shortly after. If you want extra peace of mind, Settings lets you overwrite temporary files (1, 3, or 7 passes) before they're deleted, though modern phone storage limits how much that really adds, as the app itself is upfront about. Clear Cache on Startup automatically clears out anything older than 24 hours, and you can always clear it by hand from Settings too.

When You Uninstall Redact

Android keeps every app's private data in its own sandbox, so uninstalling Redact wipes all of it instantly, your settings, including whether crash reporting was on, and anything left in the temporary cache. None of it gets backed up to the cloud first, since we've turned that off. The exception is your actual photos and videos: anything already saved to Pictures/Redact or Movies/Redact is yours, just like photos from your camera, and uninstalling the app won't touch them.

If you'd turned crash reporting on at some point, any reports already sent may still be sitting in Sentry's systems for as long as their own retention policy keeps them. You're welcome to reach out to Sentry directly, or just email us using the details in Section 16, and we'll help however we can.

10. Security

Because everything happens locally and there's no account system or database anywhere, there's simply no central server holding your photos, videos, or personal information for anyone to break into. The only network connection Redact ever makes is the single, opt-in diagnostic channel from Section 6, and that's encrypted over HTTPS.

Redact also blocks all unencrypted network traffic outright (usesCleartextTraffic="false") and turns off Android's automatic app backup (allowBackup="false"), so nothing can be pulled out through a device backup. There's no password, PIN, or credential stored anywhere, since there's no account to begin with. And because the full source code is public, you don't have to take our word for any of this, anyone can go check for themselves.

13. Do Not Track Signals

Some web browsers send a "Do Not Track" signal to websites. Since Redact is a phone app rather than a website, it has no way to even receive that signal, and there's no tracking going on for it to switch off anyway. If you've turned on optional crash reporting, switching it back off in Settings does the same job, it stops any further data in its tracks right away.

14. Your Rights

Legal Basis for Processing (GDPR)

Wherever the GDPR applies, the only processing Redact ever does, sending that optional diagnostic data to Sentry, is based entirely on your consent, given the moment you switch on Send Crash Reports. You can withdraw that consent at any time by switching it off again, which stops any further processing immediately. It doesn't retroactively affect the lawfulness of anything already sent before you withdrew it.

European Economic Area, UK, and Switzerland (GDPR)

If you're in the EEA, UK, or Switzerland, data protection law gives you the right to access, correct, delete, restrict, or move your personal data, and to object to how it's used. Since Redact doesn't collect or process your personal data in the first place, most of these rights simply won't have anything to act on, there's nothing sitting around to request. If you think some data about you has reached us anyway, say, through an email you sent, just get in touch and we'll sort it out quickly.

International Data Transfers

If you turn on crash reporting, that limited diagnostic data from Section 6 might be processed by Sentry (Functional Software, Inc.) on servers outside your own country, including in the United States. This only ever happens if you've opted in, and it only ever covers that same scrubbed technical data, never your photos, videos, or identity. Sentry's own privacy policy explains the safeguards they use for transfers like this.

California (CCPA/CPRA)

Redact doesn't sell or share personal information, and in the normal course of running the app, it doesn't collect personal information as California law defines it either.
